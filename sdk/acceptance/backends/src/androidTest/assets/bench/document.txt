The Harbour Market of Elmstead: a short history

Elmstead is a small town where a slow river meets a wide, shallow bay, and for most of its history the town lived by what the river brought down and what the tide carried in. The first record of a market there is a ledger from the late twelfth century, in which a monastery on the hill noted that its brothers had bought salt, eels and a barrel of tar from the "landing men" who tied their boats below the bridge. The landing men were not yet a guild. They were families who owned a boat or a share of one, and who agreed, mostly by habit, to sell on the flat stretch of shingle where the road from the hill reached the water. That stretch is still called the Landing, although the shingle was paved long ago.

By the fourteenth century the market had grown enough to need rules. A charter granted by the lord of the manor allowed a weekly market on Thursdays and a fair of three days at midsummer, and in return the town paid a yearly rent of two pounds of pepper and a silver cup. The charter also fixed the weights and measures: a standard bushel of oak was kept in the church porch, and any trader whose measure was found short could be fined, or made to stand in the square with the false measure hung around his neck. The bushel survives, and is shown to visitors on the first Thursday of the month, when a volunteer in a wool cap explains that the town's disputes were settled, for several hundred years, by tipping a basket of barley into it.

The harbour itself changed more slowly than the market. The bay is shallow, and every century or so the channel drifts and the quay has to be moved or rebuilt. The wooden quay of the fifteenth century was replaced by a stone one in 1688, after a winter storm carried off half the old planking together with two warehouses and a net loft. The stone quay was paid for by a tax on herring, which the fishermen resented and the merchants praised, and which was quietly dropped when the herring left the bay a generation later. Nobody in Elmstead agrees on why the herring left. Some blame the dredging of the channel, some blame the new mill upstream that fouled the water with dye, and some say that fish, like customers, simply go where they are better treated.

The eighteenth century was the market's richest age. A turnpike road reached the town in 1752, and for the first time a cart of fresh fish could be in the county town by evening. The traders built the arcaded Market Hall that still stands in the square, with its open ground floor for stalls and a long room above where the corn factors met to settle prices. The upper room has a painted ceiling, commissioned in 1771, which shows the four seasons as four women carrying the town's goods: a basket of eels for winter, a bundle of reeds for spring, a sheaf of barley for summer and a net full of herring for autumn. Visitors usually notice that the herring woman has two left hands. The painter, it is said, was paid by the day and ran out of patience.

The nineteenth century brought the railway, and with it the first real decline. The line was meant to pass through Elmstead, but the engineers found the marshy ground by the bay too costly to cross, and the station was built two miles inland, on higher ground, at a place that had until then been a single farm. Within thirty years the farm had become a village, then a rival market, and the landing men found that the buyers who once walked down the hill now waited at the station for goods to be brought to them. The Thursday market shrank to a handful of stalls. The fair of midsummer survived only because the town council, in 1884, voted a small grant to keep it, on the grounds that a town without a fair is not quite a town.

The twentieth century was kinder in unexpected ways. Two wars emptied the harbour of its young men and filled the Market Hall with sandbags, ration books and, for a few winters, a communal kitchen that served soup to anyone who brought a bowl. After the second war the fishing fleet never recovered, and by the nineteen sixties only four boats still worked out of the Landing. It was then that a retired schoolteacher, Margaret Hale, began to write a weekly note about the market in the parish magazine, and then to organise it. She persuaded the council to stop charging stall rents, found a baker, a cheesemaker and a man who sold secondhand books, and promised each of them a dry corner under the arcade. Within five years the Thursday market had twice as many stalls as at any time since the railway, and the fair of midsummer had a brass band.

Today the Harbour Market runs every Thursday and Saturday from eight in the morning until two in the afternoon, and on the first Sunday of each month there is a larger market with food stalls along the quay. About sixty traders hold permits. A permit is granted by a small committee of residents, and it is renewed each spring, which means that a trader who sells poor goods or who is rude to customers can simply be left off the list without any formal charge. The committee keeps a waiting list of about forty names, and the longest wait, for a place selling fresh bread, is seven years. Fishmongers have a reserved row nearest the water, a custom that dates from the days when the catch was landed directly onto the stalls, and the rule still stands even though the fish now arrives in refrigerated vans.

The market's money matters to the town in a way that visitors rarely see. The permits and a small levy on the Sunday stalls pay for the upkeep of the quay, the lighting of the arcade, and the winter repair of the Market Hall roof, which leaks above the painted ceiling in a way that every generation promises to fix. A survey done by the town council in 2019 found that about one household in five relied on the market for some part of its income, whether as a trader, a cook, a driver, or the person who sweeps the square at night. The same survey found, to nobody's surprise, that the most common complaint among residents was the parking, and the second most common was the gulls.

The gulls deserve a paragraph of their own. They are herring gulls, large and unafraid, and they have learned that the stalls nearest the water are the easiest to rob. Traders keep a long stick under the counter, and children are warned to eat their chips with their backs to a wall. In 2016 the council tried a plastic owl, then a recorded hawk call, then a trained falcon that came for three weeks and caught nothing but a hat. The council now accepts the gulls as part of the market, and a stallholder who loses a sandwich to one is entitled, by an unwritten rule, to a free cup of tea from the neighbouring stall.

Looking ahead, the town faces two plain problems. The first is the sea, which is rising slowly and has already topped the quay twice in the last five winters. The council has commissioned a plan to raise the quay by half a metre and to put a flap gate on the drain that runs under the square, and the cost is about the same as the town's whole yearly budget. The second problem is the market's own success: on summer Saturdays the square is so crowded that the older residents no longer go, and the traders who built the market's reputation complain that the new visitors photograph the stalls and buy nothing. The committee has discussed limiting the number of visitors, and decided against it, on the grounds that a market which turns people away has stopped being a market. Instead it plans a second, quieter market on Wednesday afternoons, aimed at the town's own residents, with the same traders and the same prices, and with the gulls, it is hoped, unable to tell the difference.

There is also the matter of the people who make the market work without ever standing behind a stall. The square is swept at six every Thursday and Saturday by a crew of three, who start with the fishmongers' row and work uphill because the water runs that way when they hose it down. The electricity for the arcade is switched on by the caretaker of the Market Hall, a man who has held the keys for twenty-six years and who is said to know every trader's opening time and every trader's excuse for being late. The market's accounts are kept by a retired bank clerk who is paid in cheese, and the committee's minutes are written, by long tradition, in pencil, so that a decision can be corrected without anyone having to admit that it was wrong.

The traders, for their part, have a quiet code of their own. A stallholder who sells out early will send customers to a neighbour who still has stock, and a trader who is ill will find that someone has opened the stall for him and kept the takings in a tin. Newcomers are watched for a season. They are not treated unkindly, but nobody expects them to last until they have been through a wet February, when the wind comes straight off the bay, the arcade is half empty, and the only customers are the ones who need bread more than they need conversation. Those who stay through that month are given, without ceremony, a hook of their own on the wall behind the cheese stall, where they hang their coats.
